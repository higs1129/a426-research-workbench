# Structure And Scientific Prose

## Establish The Paper's Spine

Before drafting sections, answer:

1. What physical system is studied?
2. What observable is normally used?
3. Where and why does that observable lose validity or usefulness?
4. What complementary information remains available?
5. What is proposed, predicted, and demonstrated?

These answers form the paper's spine. Sections should advance it rather than repeat it.

## Paragraph Architecture

Use a five-part paragraph when helpful:

1. **Anchor:** connect to the preceding result or physical setting.
2. **Question:** identify the specific issue the paragraph resolves.
3. **Mechanism:** explain the causal or mathematical relation.
4. **Evidence:** point to a calculation, measurement, or source.
5. **Consequence:** state why the next step follows.

Do not mechanically include all five as separate sentences. The test is whether the paragraph has one purpose and leaves a clear next question.

## Introduction Logic

An introduction normally moves through:

- the physical platform and established capability;
- the performance quantity that matters for the present problem;
- the mechanism creating the limitation;
- prior solution families and their remaining tradeoffs;
- the present strategy, evidence, and bounded contribution.

Avoid literature catalogues. Group prior work by the physical strategy used and explain what each strategy changes in the measurement.

## Results Logic

Do not write "Fig. 1 shows..., Fig. 2 shows..." as the governing structure. State the scientific development, then cite the figure as evidence. A good sequence is:

`physical picture -> model prediction -> apparatus/calibration -> observation -> comparison -> implication`

If two sections repeat the same conceptual contrast, merge them or assign one a qualitative role and the other a quantitatively distinct question.

### Observation--Mechanism--Measurement Sequence

For an experimental result that changes regime, organize the prose around one
physical question rather than around panel order:

`observed relation -> regime boundary -> physical mechanism -> information retained in a complementary observable -> bounded measurement conclusion`

Use these rules:

1. Let the experimentally varied quantity provide the visible progression.
2. Connect the low-amplitude trend to an established theoretical scaling before
   reporting the inferred sensitivity or calibration.
3. When the first observable turns over, state the resulting measurement
   ambiguity, not merely that the curve becomes nonlinear.
4. Use prior theory to explain the turnover once; do not rederive or repeatedly
   paraphrase the same mechanism in the Results.
5. Describe the complementary observable positively: what visible physical
   change occurs, why it occurs, and what quantity can be recovered from it.
   Avoid organizing the paragraph around what the analysis does not require.
6. Make the next paragraph answer the unresolved measurement question left by
   the previous one.
7. Close by reconnecting all observables to one underlying physical quantity
   and state the demonstrated range together with its experimental boundary.

This pattern is not a license to force continuity. Distinguish a shared sampled
setting from a universal threshold, and distinguish a source-limited endpoint
from an intrinsic limit.

## Language Tests

- **System-first test:** Does the reader know what object, degree of freedom, and observable are being discussed before a limitation appears?
- **Causality test:** Can "because," "therefore," and "whereas" be defended physically?
- **Remove-citation test:** If citations vanish, does the prose still form an argument rather than a list?
- **Remove-figure-number test:** If "Fig. X" is removed, does the paragraph still have a scientific subject?
- **Core-sentence test:** Can a reader identify the one sentence the paper wants remembered?

## Terminology

Build a small project glossary for quantities that could be confused. For each term record:

- symbol;
- physical meaning;
- units;
- measurement or extraction route;
- preferred noun and verb;
- terms to avoid.

Prefer the terminology used consistently in authoritative papers from the same subfield, but do not imitate phrases that obscure the present mechanism.
